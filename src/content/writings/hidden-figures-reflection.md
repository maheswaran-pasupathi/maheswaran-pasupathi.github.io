Some movies entertain us for a couple of hours and end with the credits. Some stay with us a little longer.

*Hidden Figures* was one of those movies for me.

If you have not watched it yet, I would simply say: **give it a chance.**

It is a feel-good movie, but not in a superficial way. It gives confidence. It makes you think. And I believe there is something in it for almost everyone — engineers, students, leaders, men, women, or simply anyone who has ever had a dream and felt that the world was trying to define their limits.

The movie follows **Katherine Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan and Mary Jackson**, three women working at NASA during the American space program. I do not want to explain too much about the story here. I would rather leave that experience to you.

**Go and watch it.**

What I want to write about is what the movie made me think about.

## Reaching for space while struggling on the ground

One of the early scenes immediately caught my attention. The three women are travelling when a police officer approaches them. There is fear and uncertainty in that interaction. At the same time, these are women working for NASA — an organization trying to solve some of the most difficult scientific and engineering problems of that era.

That contradiction stayed with me.

Human beings were trying to reach beyond Earth, yet on the ground society was still struggling to treat another human being equally. We could think about orbital mechanics, re-entry, trajectories and spaceflight, but something as basic as equality was still difficult.

There is something uncomfortable about that.

> **What are the things that we consider completely normal now that people fifty years from today may find impossible to understand?**

Race was one boundary. Gender was another. In different parts of the world, the boundary may be caste, ethnicity, social background, education, language, nationality, or something else.

The boundaries change. The human tendency to create them seems much slower to disappear.

## Katherine — when your capability is questioned before your work is seen

Katherine Johnson's part of the story affected me in another way.

Imagine walking into a room where, even before you speak, people have already made assumptions about what you are capable of — not because they have seen your work, but because of your gender, colour, background, or simply because you are not part of the group that normally occupies that room.

That is difficult to imagine if we have never experienced it ourselves.

My path may be different. **Our paths may all be different.**

I have not experienced the same struggles these women faced. But the broader idea of being placed into a box is something many of us may recognize.

Sometimes the world sees your previous title, specialization, degree or past experience and quickly decides what you can and cannot do.

I work in engineering and computational simulation. Much of my career has been associated with combustion and CFD. But underneath an application such as combustion are fundamentals — fluid mechanics, heat transfer, thermodynamics, numerical methods and engineering judgment.

So when I think about moving into another technology — battery thermal management, electrification, e-motors, vehicle thermal systems, AI or something completely new — I sometimes ask myself:

> **How much of my future should be defined by what I have already done?**

There is a strong desire in me to prove that the foundation can travel.

Perhaps that is one small place where I can connect my own journey with the movie. Not the magnitude of their struggle, but the feeling of wanting the work itself to speak louder than the label attached to you.

## Mary — opportunity, qualification, and the excuses we make today

Mary Jackson's journey gave me a different question.

She knows what she wants to become. She has the capability. But there is an eligibility barrier standing in front of her. To move forward, she needs access to education that the system itself makes difficult for her to obtain.

Watching that made me think about our world today.

Many of us now have access to opportunities that would have been unimaginable a few decades ago. We can take a university course online, learn programming from home, earn certifications while working, and reach books, research papers, simulations, open-source software, online communities and now AI tools almost instantly.

Yet how often do we tell ourselves:

*"I don't have time."*  
*"I'll do it after this project."*  
*"Maybe next month."*  
*"I'll learn it when my job demands it."*

I am asking this question to myself as much as anyone reading this.

Am I learning only when my career forces me to learn? Or am I deliberately preparing myself for the person I want to become?

Mary had to fight simply for the opportunity to enter the classroom. Today, many of us already have the classroom in our pocket.

> **Are we using it?**

## Dorothy — seeing the technology coming around the corner

Of the three, I probably connect most closely with **Dorothy Vaughan**.

She sees a technological change approaching. A new computing machine is coming, and she understands something important: the nature of the work is going to change.

Her response is not to complain about the machine.

She learns.

And what I particularly liked is that she does not think only about herself. She prepares the people around her too.

That is powerful.

We are living through our own version of such a transition now. AI is changing engineering. Electrification is changing the automotive industry. Automation is changing repetitive engineering work. Data-driven methods, reduced-order models, surrogate modelling and new computational tools are changing how analysis itself is performed.

Some roles will change. Some skills will lose value. New ones will become important.

> **When we see the change coming, do we resist it, or do we prepare for it?**

And there is a second question which I think is even more important:

> **If we manage to prepare ourselves, do we also help the people around us prepare?**

That part connects strongly with the kind of engineer — and perhaps the kind of person — I want to become.

Success becomes more meaningful when it is not only about escaping the problem ourselves. If our learning can pull a few other people forward, the impact becomes much larger.

## A leadership lesson hidden inside a space program

There is another part of the movie that stayed with me from a leadership perspective.

The people leading the space program are dealing with extraordinarily complicated problems: timelines, calculations, engineering uncertainty, national pressure and human lives. When you are solving problems of that magnitude, it is very easy to become completely focused on the mission.

But underneath that mission, one person may be struggling with something painfully basic. Sometimes leadership only becomes aware of it when the situation reaches a breaking point.

That made me reflect on something.

As leaders, we should not always wait for the breaking point.

A project can be technically successful while something underneath it is quietly failing. Someone may not have the right opportunity. Someone may be afraid to speak. Someone may be carrying an unreasonable burden. Someone may simply feel that nobody is seeing them.

Leadership cannot mean only asking:

> **"Did we deliver?"**

Sometimes it also needs to ask:

> **"What is happening to the people while we are delivering?"**

You can be running a space program and still miss what is happening a few metres away from you. That is perhaps one of the quieter lessons I took from the film.

## Not everyone is simply good or evil

Another thing I appreciated is that the movie did not leave me thinking only in terms of heroes and villains.

Human beings are more complicated than that.

People can follow unfair systems without necessarily waking up every morning intending to hurt somebody. They can care deeply about their own family, children and friends while still accepting unfairness toward someone outside their circle.

Most of us know how to love. We simply draw boundaries around where that love and empathy should stop — family, community, language, gender, nationality, race, organization, sometimes even education or professional hierarchy.

Perhaps progress happens when those circles slowly become wider.

## And then there is life outside engineering

There is another dimension of the movie that I don't want to leave out.

We engineers sometimes become deeply absorbed in difficult problems — simulations, deadlines, design decisions, validation, programs, technology and career.

We may be solving something extremely sophisticated, but when we close the laptop, we are still human.

Someone may be waiting at home.

A partner who needs our presence.

Children who do not really care what project we delivered — they simply want some time with us.

Parents who are getting older and may not always be available when our calendar finally becomes free.

I feel this personally.

We spend enormous energy optimizing engineering systems. Sometimes we should ask whether we are also paying attention to the system called life.

There will always be another project. Another deadline. Another technology to learn. Another career milestone.

But some moments do not return.

**No technical achievement completely replaces human connection.**

## What does a successful career finally mean?

This movie also pushed me toward a question that I still cannot answer completely.

We live in a very fast world. Every day we see someone's promotion on LinkedIn. Someone changes companies. Someone finishes a doctorate. Someone gets a patent. Someone receives an award. Someone's salary jumps.

And without realizing it, we start measuring our careers in very short windows: this appraisal cycle, this promotion, this year, this paycheck.

Those things matter. I am not pretending they don't.

But I am increasingly interested in a much longer question:

> **At the end of my career, what would make me feel that the journey was worth it?**

I do not have the final answer yet. But one part is becoming clearer.

I would like my work to have helped more than just myself.

Maybe that means helping younger engineers. Maybe contributing knowledge. Maybe teaching. Maybe research. Maybe building something that survives after I move on. Maybe helping a community adapt to a technology shift rather than simply adapting alone.

I am still figuring that out.

And perhaps that is okay.

## A small time machine for an engineer

There was also something personally enjoyable about watching *Hidden Figures* as someone who works in computational engineering.

The terminology itself feels like a small time machine.

"Computers" were once people performing calculations. Programming was emerging. Computational resources were precious. Calculations that can now be performed in seconds demanded enormous human effort.

Today, we casually work with computational power that engineers of that era could hardly have imagined — simulation, high-performance computing, optimization, machine learning and AI.

Sometimes watching the movie made me appreciate how far the engineering world has travelled.

If you work in engineering, aerodynamics, computational science or programming, you may connect with that aspect very naturally.

If you don't, don't worry.

**The technology is only the background. The human emotions carry the movie.**

## After the movie ended

When the movie finished, I did not come away with one clean lesson.

I came away with questions.

Am I preparing myself for the technologies coming around the corner?

Am I learning because I am curious, or only when my job forces me to?

Am I helping the people around me grow?

Am I sometimes judging somebody through a label before understanding their capability?

If I lead people, am I paying enough attention to what is happening underneath the work?

Am I building a career that I will eventually consider meaningful?

And while building all of that, am I giving enough time to the people who matter to me?

I still do not have perfect answers.

Maybe I don't need them immediately.

For now, I am simply sitting with the movie and the questions it left behind.

Perhaps that is one sign of a movie worth watching.

If you have already watched *Hidden Figures*, I would genuinely like to know what stayed with you. And if you watch it after reading this, come back and tell me what you saw in it.

Your reflection may be completely different from mine. **That is the interesting part.**

If this reflection reaches you through LinkedIn, please add your thoughts there. If you know me personally, a message or email is equally welcome. I would like these film notes to become conversations rather than one-way reviews.

These are simply the thoughts the movie left with me.

**Thank you for spending a few minutes reading them.**
